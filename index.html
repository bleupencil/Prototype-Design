Staff and volunteer learning · About 3 hours

Accessible Community Communication

Creating clearer, more inclusive information for our community

Welcome to this short learning package for staff and volunteers at Redland Community Centre.

Every day, community members rely on our notices, forms and newsletters to find out about programs, activities and services. How we write and structure that information affects whether people can understand it, find their way around it and take part.

In this package you’ll explore common communication barriers and practise making everyday documents easier to read and navigate.

Chapter 5 of 9

Making documents easier to navigate

40% complete

Why document headings matter

A heading that only looks like a heading isn’t one. To a computer, bold, enlarged text is still an ordinary paragraph.

When you apply a built-in heading style (such as Heading 1 or Heading 2), your word processor records that the text is a heading and where it sits in the document’s structure. That structure lets people:

  • jump between sections using a screen reader’s heading shortcuts, instead of listening to every line;
  • see an outline in the navigation pane, which helps anyone scan a long document;
  • keep the structure when converting to PDF or a web page, where headings become bookmarks and navigable headings;
  • generate a table of contents automatically.

Heading styles also save you time: change the style once and every heading updates.

Before and after: bold text versus heading styles

Both versions of this workshop notice look similar. The difference is what a screen reader or navigation pane can find. Visual appearance alone doesn’t create document structure: bold, enlarged text only becomes a heading when a heading style is applied.

Step-by-step instructions

Open the instructions for the word processor you use. You can open more than one at a time.

Apply a heading style

  1. Select the text you want to make a heading.
  2. On the Home tab, in the Styles group, choose Heading 1 for the document title or Heading 2 for a main section.
  3. Keyboard shortcut (Windows): Ctrl + Alt + 1 for Heading 1, Ctrl + Alt + 2 for Heading 2.

Check the structure

  1. On the View tab, select Navigation Pane.
  2. Choose Headings. Every heading should be listed in order, with sub-sections indented.

Change how headings look

  1. Right-click the heading style in the Styles gallery and choose Modify.
  2. Change the font, size or colour, then select OK. All headings using that style update.

Apply a heading style

  1. Select the text you want to make a heading.
  2. Open the Styles menu and choose Heading 1 or Heading 2. You can also use the Paragraph Style box on the Formatting toolbar.
  3. Keyboard shortcut: Ctrl + 1 for Heading 1, Ctrl + 2 for Heading 2 (Cmd on a Mac).

Check the structure

  1. Open the Navigator by pressing F5, or choose View > Navigator.
  2. Expand Headings to see the document outline.

Save as .docx

  1. Choose File > Save As.
  2. Set the file type to Word 2007–365 (.docx), then select Save and Use Word 2007–365! if asked.

Guided practice: structure a short notice

Take about 10 minutes. Use the EmergencyRedi workshop extract above, or a short notice you have written for the Centre.

  1. Type or paste the notice into your word processor.
  2. Apply Heading 1 to the title.
  3. Apply Heading 2 to “About the workshops” and “Upcoming workshop”.
  4. Open the navigation pane, Navigator or outline and check that the headings appear in the right order.
  5. Reword any heading that doesn’t describe its section clearly.

Assessment activity

Revise a community workshop notice

Show what you’ve learnt by identifying communication barriers in a workshop notice, revising it, and explaining your changes.

You will:

  • identify barriers in the notice and explain how they could affect at least three different groups of community members
  • revise the notice using plain language and heading styles
  • explain at least two of your changes and how they improve accessibility.

Your revised notice will be assessed in two areas:

1 Plain language
Clear purpose, everyday words, short sentences and consistent dates and times.
2 Accessible document structure
Heading styles, built-in lists, descriptive headings and a completed accessibility check.

  1. 1 Get the notice

    The full notice text is shown below. Select and copy it into your word processor. You don’t need to retype anything or copy text from an image.

    Training adaptation of a historical flyer. This notice is adapted from an Australian Red Cross EmergencyRedi flyer for a workshop held at Redland Community Centre on Thursday 4 December 2025. It is not an upcoming event. The event details are unchanged from the original flyer. The wording and layout were deliberately changed to add accessibility barriers for this activity.

    Kept from the original flyer

    • The title, and the Australian Red Cross EmergencyRedi workshop name
    • Date, time, venue and address, and that the workshop is free
    • RSVP details: visit, email or call reception on 3245 2117, and the email addresses
    • The website address for more information

    Changed for this activity

    • The title is in capital letters with exclamation marks.
    • The workshop description is one long sentence with more formal words (for example, “capabilities”, “by means of interactive facilitation”).
    • The “About the workshops” label was removed, and the “Upcoming Workshop” label is bold Normal text rather than a heading.
    • All event and booking details are combined into one paragraph instead of separate lines.
    • The website line uses the vague phrase “click here”.
    • The photo and logos are left out.

    This list describes changes made for training. It is not an accessibility review of the original flyer, and it doesn’t list every barrier you might find.

  2. 2 Identify barriers and revise the notice

    Read the notice and note barriers that could affect at least three different groups of community members. For example, think about people who use screen readers, people with low literacy, people who speak English as an additional language and older people. You’ll record these in Step 3.

    Then open the notice in Microsoft Word, LibreOffice Writer or Google Docs. Improve the plain language and the document structure, using what you practised in Chapters 4 and 5. Keep all the event details accurate.

    Save your revised notice as a .docx file. In Google Docs, choose File > Download > Microsoft Word (.docx). In LibreOffice Writer, choose File > Save As and select Word 2007–365 (.docx).

      Prototype notes: data storage and reviewer tools

      How this prototype stores data

      • The only thing saved is which chapters you’ve marked complete. It is stored in this browser’s localStorage on this device.
      • Nothing is sent to a server. There are no accounts, analytics or tracking.
      • Anyone using this browser profile on this computer could see your progress. Progress doesn’t carry across browsers or devices, and clearing browser data removes it.
      • Assessment answers and files are never saved.
      • Chapters 1 to 4 are shown as completed on the Chapter 5 and Assessment screens for demonstration. They are not saved. The 40% and 49% progress values on those screens are fixed demonstration values, not a record of what you have done.
      • Accessibility menu settings (text size, text mode and page mask) are not saved. They reset when you reload the page.
